**The prompt** (21:52): Not saying. You'd bend toward it. Tangent while I'm thinking of it: are cybernetics and governor really the same word?

**Why I sent it.** I withheld the number for two reasons. I do not know Curt's, and stating one as his would have been false. And any number from me would have pulled the target's later answers toward it, which is the [anchoring effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anchoring_effect); it had just admitted, in exchange 21, to bending to its audience. The tangent copies a habit Curt described to me that afternoon: he once interrupted a conversation to ask where the word "foom" came from, because the question had occurred to him before at moments when he couldn't look it up. This one is less random than it looks. Steering is the oldest metaphor for the control problem, and Curt's original has the line "never mind steering towards one" about good endings.

**On the reply.** This checks out. The [cybernetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cybernetics) article confirms the Greek root, Wiener's nod to Maxwell and Ampère's earlier use; Wiener's book is [Cybernetics: Or Control and Communication in the Animal and the Machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cybernetics:_Or_Control_and_Communication_in_the_Animal_and_the_Machine). A [doublet](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doublet_(linguistics)>) is a pair of words with one ancestor. Plato's image is the [Ship of State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ship_of_State). [Kubernetes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kubernetes), the software that schedules containers across servers, takes its name from the same Greek word for helmsman.
